How to read the clouds

Electrons don't orbit like tiny planets — they live in fuzzy clouds called orbitals, and the cloud above shows where the electron is likely to be found. Here's the secret: every atom in the universe uses these same shapes — carbon's 2p and gold's 5d look alike because the maths behind them depends only on the quantum numbers, not on the element.

  • Blue and rose show the wavefunction's sign. Like a wave, an orbital has positive and negative regions. Where they meet, the value is zero — a node, a place the electron can never be.
  • Radial nodes are gaps between shells (a 4s orbital has three!), angular nodes are the flat cuts that split lobes. The label under each cloud counts them.
  • One wavefunction, two drawings. dots samples the electron's probability dot by dot — the fastest drawing. glow adds the same probability along each line of sight, like a long-exposure photo. The dials tune the glow; pure glow puts them back.
  • One honest caveat: these are hydrogen's exact orbitals used as shapes for all atoms. In heavy atoms, the many electrons screen each other and squeeze the clouds a little — the shapes survive, the sizes shift.
  • Isotopes: same element, different neutrons. The bars list every isotope ever observed — the NUBASE2020 evaluation served by the IAEA Live Chart of Nuclides. Green is stable, and the bar's height is the isotope's share of the natural element; red is radioactive, including the rare natural ones (all of bismuth is red: bismuth-209 does decay, but takes 2.01×10¹⁹ years to do it). Click any bar to rebuild the nucleus — only the nucleus changes, the electron clouds stay exactly the same.
